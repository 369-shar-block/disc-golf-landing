<Answer>

Disc golf 3D form analysis turns a video of your throw into a moving 3D body that you can rotate and watch from any angle. Computer vision estimates where your joints are in three dimensions in every frame, from one ordinary phone clip, with no markers or sensors. The payoff is seeing views you did not film: a top-down look at your pull path, your shoulder turn against your hips, and the depth of your reach-back. It is strong for positions and timing, weaker for exact depth, and it does not measure the disc.

</Answer>

Flat video has a basic problem: it only shows what crosses the camera's view. Anything moving toward or away from the lens gets squashed. That is why a side-on clip can hide rounding completely, and why form check threads so often end with "film it from behind too." 3D form analysis is a way around that. You film once, then move the camera after the fact.

Full disclosure: I build Disc Golf Form Analyzer, which has a 3D feature called 3D Throw. This guide explains how 3D disc golf throw analysis works in general, including what it gets wrong, and I'll point out where the app fits.

## What 3D form analysis actually is

A normal video is a stack of flat pictures. A 3D reconstruction is a stack of 3D poses: for each frame, an estimate of where your hips, knees, ankles, shoulders, elbows, wrists and head sit in space. Play those poses in order and you get a figure that moves exactly like you did, which you can view from anywhere.

That is the same idea as lab motion capture, where reflective markers are stuck to the body and a ring of cameras tracks them. The difference is that markerless systems skip the markers and the camera ring. They infer the body from the image itself.

### How one phone video becomes a 3D body

In plain terms, the process has three steps.

1. **Find the person.** The software locates you in each frame and separates you from the background.
2. **Estimate the 3D pose.** A body tracking model, trained on many examples of people with known 3D positions, looks at the image and estimates where each joint is in three dimensions. It is using the same cues you use when you look at a photo and can tell someone's arm is reaching toward you: proportions, overlap, shading and the way limbs foreshorten.
3. **Join the frames into one motion.** Frame-by-frame estimates are noisy, so the software tracks the same person through the clip and smooths the motion so the figure moves continuously instead of jittering.

The phrase you'll see for this is 3D pose estimation or 3D body reconstruction. 2D pose estimation, the skeleton drawn on top of your video, is the simpler cousin: it finds joints on the flat image only. I cover the 2D side in [how disc golf form analysis software works](/blog/how-disc-golf-form-analysis-software-works).

### How it compares with lab motion capture

Phone-based tracking is useful, but it is not a lab. A study comparing three popular markerless 2D tracking tools against marker-based motion capture found their joint centers were "not yet consistently comparable," with hip and knee differences of roughly 30 to 50 mm ([Needham et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8526586/)). A research system that uses two or more phones, OpenCap, measured joint angles with a 4.5 degree mean absolute error against lab capture ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1011462)).

A single camera is harder still. A 2025 review of single-camera 3D pose estimation names "depth ambiguity and occlusion" as the key challenges of a one-view setup ([Guo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12031093/)). Keep that in mind and you will read a 3D replay sensibly: trust clear shapes and sequences, not tiny differences.

## What 3D shows that 2D video hides

This is where 3D earns its place. These are the checks a single flat clip struggles with.

### Your pull path, from above

Rounding is a sideways curve: the disc swings out away from your chest instead of pulling across it. From the side, that curve moves toward and away from the camera, so it barely shows. From above, it is obvious. A top-down view plus a hand trail (a line that traces where your throwing hand traveled) draws the pull path as a shape you can see at a glance: a fairly straight line along the target line, or a wide arc.

If rounding is your issue, the full diagnosis is in [how to stop rounding your backhand](/blog/how-to-stop-rounding-disc-golf-backhand).

### Hip and shoulder separation

Coaches talk about the hips leading and the shoulders staying closed a beat longer. From the side you can see some of this; from above you can see the hip line and the shoulder line as two separate bars and watch the gap between them open and close. If both bars turn together, the body is rotating as one block. Proximal-to-distal sequencing, where bigger segments near the center of the body start the motion and the arm finishes it, is the classic model for throwing ([Putnam 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(93)90084-R)). Top-down 3D is the most direct way a phone gives you to see it.

### The depth of your reach-back

Is the disc going back along the target line, or out to the side and away from your body? Did your shoulders actually turn, or did the arm just swing back? From the front or the side these are hard to separate. From above and from behind, they are clear. More on what the reach-back should do in [disc golf reach-back](/blog/disc-golf-reach-back).

### The brace and where your weight goes

A side view of the 3D figure shows whether your front leg firms up and stops your forward drift, or keeps bending and sliding. Rotating to a front view shows whether your hips stay stacked over the plant foot or fall away. If the brace is your question, [hips, brace and weight shift](/blog/disc-golf-hips-brace-weight-shift) goes deeper.

### Angles you forgot to film

The most practical benefit is simple: you filmed side-on, but your question needs a behind view. With 3D you rotate the figure instead of going back to the field.

| What you want to check | Best 3D view | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Rounding / pull path | Top-down, with hand trail | Straight line along the target line vs a wide arc |
| Hip lead and shoulder separation | Top-down | Hip line turning before the shoulder line |
| Reach-back depth and coil | Top-down and behind | Disc back along the line, shoulders turned away |
| Power pocket | Behind or front | Disc close to the chest, elbow leading |
| Brace and plant | Side | Front leg firming up, body stopping over it |
| Early rotation (opening up) | Front | Chest facing the target before the plant |
| Balance at the finish | Front and side | Head and hips stacked over the front foot |

For a full view-by-view and phase-by-phase routine, see [how to read your disc golf throw in 3D](/blog/how-to-read-your-disc-golf-throw-in-3d).

## The honest limits

3D replays look convincing, which makes it easy to over-trust them. Here is what they cannot do.

### One camera guesses depth

Movement across the camera's view is measured directly from the image. Movement toward or away from the camera has to be inferred. So the axis pointing at the lens is always the least certain. If you film side-on, the sideways direction of your pull (toward and away from the camera) is the estimated one. That matters for rounding: a top-down view built from a side-on clip is a good first look, but if the answer is close, film a second clip from behind and compare.

Absolute sizes and speeds are also less certain than angles and timing. Treat "my shoulders turned further on throw B than throw A" as meaningful and "my hand moved 0.4 m" as a rough guess.

### Blur at the fastest moment

The hand and disc are moving fastest right at release, which is exactly when frames blur. A review of 2D video analysis in sport notes that images need to be "sharp and motion blur-free, especially in high speed motions" ([Pueo 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3010/301049620005.pdf)). At 30 frames per second, a lot happens between two frames at the hit, so the wrist and hand positions right at release are the shakiest part of any reconstruction. Bright light helps the most.

### Hidden body parts

When your throwing arm passes behind your torso, the camera cannot see it, and the software has to fill the gap from what came before and after. That is occlusion, the second challenge the review above names. Expect the arm to be least reliable when it is hidden from your filming angle.

### No disc data

3D form analysis reconstructs your body. It knows nothing about the disc: no speed, no spin, no nose angle, no wobble. Those are what sensor discs measure; TechDisc's metrics, for example, include speed, spin, nose angle, launch angle, hyzer angle and wobble ([TechDisc metrics](https://techdisc.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/153000074253-techdisc-metrics)). If your question is "why is my disc flipping," the disc's numbers matter. If it is "what is my body doing wrong," the body is where to look. Many players benefit from both.

### It does not replace judgment

A 3D figure shows you what happened. It does not, on its own, tell you which difference matters. That is the job of a coach, a written breakdown, or your own checklist.

## How to film for disc golf 3D form analysis

The reconstruction can only be as good as the clip. Most problems come from the filming, not the software.

1. **One person in frame.** A friend walking behind you can confuse the tracking.
2. **Whole body, the whole time.** Head to plant foot, from the start of the run-up through the follow-through.
3. **Steady phone.** Tripod, bag or a still hand. No panning after the disc.
4. **Side-on is a good default.** It shows the stride, plant and brace clearly, and 3D lets you rotate to other views. If your main question is rounding or the power pocket, film from behind as well.
5. **Good, even light.** Sun behind the camera, not behind you.
6. **Trim to the throw.** A short clip of just the throw keeps the build focused. In Disc Golf Form Analyzer, 3D Throw accepts clips from 2 to 30 seconds.
7. **Several throws.** One throw can be a fluke. Build a few and look for what repeats.

The full setup, including camera height and distance, is in [how to film your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis).

## What to look at first in a 3D replay

If you open a 3D throw and do not know where to start, this order works.

1. **Watch once at full speed** from the default view. Get the overall rhythm.
2. **Switch to top-down and play at 1/4 speed.** Watch the hand trail. Straight or curved?
3. **Scrub to the release marker** and check where the hand is relative to your chest.
4. **Rotate to the side** and check the plant and the front leg.
5. **Rotate to the front** and check when your chest opens to the target.
6. **Pick one thing.** Not five. One fault, one drill, then film again.

Comparing two throws is often more useful than studying one. Line up a good throw and a bad one at the moment of release and the difference tends to jump out; [compare disc golf throws side by side](/blog/compare-disc-golf-throws-side-by-side) explains how to do that well.
