<Answer>
To get better at disc golf, work on five things in this order of payoff: clean up the one biggest fault in your throwing form (found on video, not by feel), putt a little almost every day, practice with a plan instead of only playing rounds, carry slower and more forgiving discs than you think you need, and make safer decisions on the course. Review your throws on video, or in 3D, every couple of weeks to check that the change is real.
</Answer>

Most players improve fast for a season and then stall. The early gains come from simply playing. The later ones come from noticing what is actually holding you back and working on that, which is harder, because the thing holding you back is usually something you cannot feel.

## Where the strokes go: a quick self-check

Before you change anything, figure out where you lose strokes. Play two or three normal rounds and note, hole by hole, which throw cost you.

| If most lost strokes come from | Start with |
|---|---|
| Drives that go short or off-line | Section 1, form |
| Missed putts inside the circle | Section 2, putting |
| Bad approach shots and upshots | Section 3, practice plan (short game blocks) |
| Trees, OB and penalty strokes | Section 5, course management |
| Discs that never fly as expected | Section 4, disc selection |

Most people find it is a mix. Pick the biggest bucket and start there.

## 1. Fix your form, one fault at a time

Form is the foundation for drives and approaches. The good news is that most form problems fall into a short list, and almost all of them show up on phone video.

**How to improve disc golf form:**

1. **Film it.** Side-on, plus behind or in front. Use the setup in [how to film your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis).
2. **Pause on key frames**: the end of the reach-back, the frame your front foot lands, the moment the disc passes your chest, release and finish.
3. **Find the earliest fault.** Throws are a chain. Classic biomechanics describes them as proximal-to-distal, with the big segments starting the motion and passing speed out to the arm and hand ([Putnam 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290%2893%2990084-R)). A fault early in the chain causes faults later, so fix the earliest one first.
4. **Drill it at low effort** until the shape holds, then add pace.

## 2. Putt more than you think you need to

Under PDGA rule 806.01, any throw from within 10 meters of the target is a putt ([PDGA rules](https://www.pdga.com/rules/official-rules-disc-golf/80601)). Nearly every hole you finish ends with one, and putting needs almost no space or time to practice. It is the most efficient improvement most players can make.

**What helps:**
- **Pick one putting style and stay with it for a while.** Spin, push and the hybrid "spush" can all work. Switching every week does not. Our [disc golf putting form](/blog/disc-golf-putting-form) guide compares them.
- **Practice short putts most.** Start close enough to make most of them, then move back only when you are making them consistently.
- **Add pressure.** Play games where a miss costs you, like "make five in a row before moving back."
- **Film your putt from the side** now and then. Balance, a consistent release height and a stable finish are all visible.

## 3. Practice with a plan, not just rounds

Rounds are fun, and you need them to learn course play. But a round gives you one throw from each spot, with no repeat and no feedback. Field work and drills give you many reps of the same thing.

A few principles from motor learning research are worth knowing:

- **Review feedback in batches.** The guidance hypothesis holds that feedback given too often, or too easy to lean on, can hurt learning ([Anderson et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1780106/)). Throw a set, then watch the set.
- **Cue the result, not the body part.** Focusing on the effect of a movement generally helps more than focusing on the movement itself ([Wulf 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2012.723728)). "Throw it through that gap" tends to work better than "keep my elbow in" once a drill feels natural.
- **Watch a good model.** A meta-analysis of observational learning found watching a model helped movement form much more than outcome scores ([Ashford, Bennett and Davids 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16709559/)). That supports comparing your throw with an ideal one.

**A simple weekly template:**

| Day | Focus | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Form drill for your one fault, then a filmed set | 30 to 45 min |
| Day 2 | Putting, short range plus a pressure game | 15 to 20 min |
| Day 3 | Approach and upshot field work | 30 min |
| Day 4 | Putting | 15 to 20 min |
| Day 5 | A round, playing safe lines | 1 to 2 hours |

Adjust it to your week. The point is that each session has one job. The full version is in our [disc golf practice plan](/blog/disc-golf-practice-plan), and drills sorted by fault are in [best disc golf drills](/blog/best-disc-golf-drills).

One rule to remember: in a sanctioned round, a practice throw costs a penalty under PDGA rule 809.03 ([PDGA rules](https://www.pdga.com/rules/official-rules-disc-golf/80903)). Do your practice before or after.

## 4. Carry discs that fit your arm

Many newer players carry drivers that need more arm speed than they have. A high-speed driver thrown too slowly does not fly as designed, so it tends to fade off early. A slower driver often goes as far or farther, and straighter. This is common coaching advice rather than a lab finding, but it is easy to test yourself on a field.

**Know the numbers.** Most discs list four flight numbers: speed, glide, turn and fade. Innova, which created the system, says to compare glide, turn and fade only between discs of the same speed ([Innova](https://www.innovadiscs.com/disc-golf-discs/flight-numbers-made-simple/)). The numbers are not standardized across brands, and the PDGA does not regulate them ([UDisc](https://udisc.com/blog/post/disc-golf-disc-numbers-what-they-mean)). Our [flight numbers explained](/blog/disc-golf-flight-numbers-explained) guide covers how to read them.

**Keep the bag small at first.** A putter, a midrange and a fairway driver will teach you most of the shots you need. Learn what each one does at different angles before adding more.

## 5. Make better decisions on the course

The cheapest strokes to save are the ones you throw away on purpose. A few habits help almost everyone:

- **Play to your typical throw, not your best one.** If you reach a gap one time in five, it is not your line.
- **Take the safe side of trouble.** When there is water or out of bounds on one side, aim your miss away from it.
- **Lay up when the putt is long.** A tap-in beats a long run at the basket that rolls away.
- **Use your most reliable disc under pressure**, even if it is not the "right" disc on paper.
- **Learn the rules that cost strokes.** Out of bounds, mandatories and stance rules are worth knowing well. The PDGA publishes the full rule book at [pdga.com/rules](https://www.pdga.com/rules).

## 6. Review your progress on video and in 3D

Feel is unreliable. A change that feels huge is often tiny on video, and a fault you are sure you fixed is often still there at full speed. So check.

Every couple of weeks, film the same throw from the same spot and compare it with an older clip. Line them up at the moment of release and look at the same frames each time.

## Set realistic expectations

There is no honest timeline for how fast anyone improves; it depends on how often you practice and what you are fixing. There is also no reliable chart of how far you "should" throw at a given level, which we explain in [how far should I throw a disc golf disc](/blog/how-far-should-i-throw-disc-golf). The benchmark that matters is you: your median drive on the same field, your make rate from the same distance, and your scores on your home course.

For distance specifically, start with [how to throw farther in disc golf](/blog/how-to-throw-farther-disc-golf).

## Your first week

1. Play one round and note which throw cost you the most strokes.
2. Film five throws from the side and five from behind.
3. Pick one fault and one drill.
4. Putt for 15 minutes on three separate days.
5. Film again next week from the same spots and compare.

That is it. One fault, a little putting, and a check on video. Repeat it, and the stalls get shorter.
