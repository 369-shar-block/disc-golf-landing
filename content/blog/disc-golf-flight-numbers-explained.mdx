<Answer>

Disc golf flight numbers are four ratings printed on most discs: Speed (1 to 14), Glide (1 to 7), Turn (+1 to -5) and Fade (0 to 5). They describe how a disc flies for a right-handed backhand thrown with enough power to reach its rated speed. The manufacturer assigns them, they are not standardized across brands, and the PDGA does not regulate them. The practical rule for picking discs: match speed to your arm, and most newer players throw lower-speed discs farther than high-speed drivers.

</Answer>

Walk into any disc shop and every disc has a little string of numbers on it, like 12 / 5 / -1 / 3. Those numbers are useful once you know what they promise and what they leave out. This guide covers both, then gets to the part that matters for your score: how to pick discs that fit the arm you have today.

## The four disc golf flight numbers

The system was created by Innova co-founder Dave Dunipace ([Innova](https://www.innovadiscs.com/disc-golf-discs/flight-numbers-made-simple/)). According to [UDisc](https://udisc.com/blog/post/disc-golf-disc-numbers-what-they-mean), the scale was finalized in 2001 and first appeared in Innova's 2002 catalog. Every number assumes a right-handed backhand (RHBH). For a left-handed backhand or a right-handed forehand, mirror the left and right directions.

| Number | Range | What it describes | Higher or more negative means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed | 1 to 14 | How fast the disc must be thrown to fly as designed | Higher speed needs more arm speed; wider rim |
| Glide | 1 to 7 | How well the disc holds lift and carries | Higher glide stays in the air longer |
| Turn | +1 to -5 | Early drift to the right during the fast part of the flight | More negative turns right more |
| Fade | 0 to 5 | The hook to the left as the disc slows down | Higher fade finishes left harder |

Read the four numbers left to right as a story of the flight. Speed tells you how hard you need to throw it. Glide tells you how much float you get. Turn is the first half of the flight, while the disc is fast. Fade is the end, when it slows down.

A few real examples from Innova's published ratings, so you can see the pattern:

- **Aviar (2 / 3 / 0 / 1):** a putter. Slow, little turn, a gentle fade.
- **Mako3 (5 / 5 / 0 / 0):** a midrange that flies very straight with no fade.
- **Leopard (6 / 5 / -2 / 1):** a fairway driver that turns right before a small fade, the kind of flight that suits a lower arm speed.
- **Destroyer (12 / 5 / -1 / 3):** a distance driver that needs a lot of arm speed and finishes with a strong fade.

### Why the S-curve happens

A disc's flight shape comes from aerodynamics, not marketing. Wind-tunnel work by Kamaruddin, Potts and Crowther identified the lift-to-drag ratio and, more importantly, the pitching moment coefficient as the key measures of how a disc performs: the ratio drives distance, while the pitching moment governs the tendency of the disc to curve away from its intended path ([Kamaruddin, Potts and Crowther 2018](https://doi.org/10.1108/AEAT-09-2016-0143)). In plain terms, the forces on a spinning disc shift as it slows down, which is why a disc can turn one way while fast and fade the other way at the end.

## Stable vs understable: what stability really means

Stability is the shorthand players use for the turn and fade numbers together.

- **Understable:** a clearly negative turn (around -2 or more) and low fade. Thrown flat at normal power, it drifts right. Good for players with less arm speed, for turnovers and for long, straight-ish flights.
- **Stable:** a turn near 0 and a mild fade. It flies fairly straight, then drifts left at the end.
- **Overstable:** little or no turn and high fade (3 or more). It resists turning and hooks left reliably, even into a headwind.

Two things change how stable a disc flies for you:

1. **Your arm speed.** A disc rated for more speed than you can give it never gets fast enough to show its turn. It just fades early. That is why a high-speed driver flies "overstable" for a beginner and "straight" for a strong thrower.
2. **Wind and wear.** Headwinds make discs act less stable; tailwinds make them act more stable. Older, beaten discs usually become less stable over time.

## Why flight numbers differ between brands

Flight numbers are a guide, not a measurement. UDisc points out that no brand publicly tests discs to set its numbers and that the ratings are not based on precise calculations ([UDisc](https://udisc.com/blog/post/disc-golf-disc-numbers-what-they-mean)). The PDGA approves discs for competition on physical properties like size, weight, shape and rim width, but it does not regulate flight numbers, so a brand can print whatever it chooses.

What that means in practice:

- **Ranges stretch.** Most brands stay on Innova's scale, but MVP rates some discs at 14.5 speed and Latitude 64 rates the Raketen and Missilen at 15.
- **Discraft adds a fifth number.** Discraft prints its own stability rating, which runs from -3 to 3, alongside the four standard numbers.
- **A "0 turn" from one brand is not the same as a "0 turn" from another.** Treat numbers as a way to compare discs within a brand's lineup, and confirm with your own field throws.

Innova adds one more rule that a lot of players miss: compare glide, turn and fade only between discs with the same speed ([Innova](https://www.innovadiscs.com/disc-golf-discs/flight-numbers-made-simple/)). A putter's "1 fade" and a distance driver's "1 fade" are not describing the same hook.

## How to pick discs for your arm

This is where flight numbers actually earn their place. The goal is a disc that you can throw at or near its rated speed, so it flies the way its numbers describe.

### Start with lower speed

UDisc's suggestion for newer players is to keep speed around 8 or lower and to test slower discs in a field, since they may give better results. If you really want a faster disc, they suggest looking at weights under 160 grams. Innova's own guide also says lower-speed discs are often a better start and that discs with more turn are easier for beginners to get a full flight from.

### Use the field test, not the shop wall

Take three discs of different speeds to an open field, for example a midrange, a fairway driver and a distance driver. Throw five of each at a comfortable, smooth effort, then walk out and look at where they landed.

| What you see | What it usually means | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| The driver lands short and hooks left right away | You are not reaching its rated speed | Drop speed, or try a more understable disc |
| The fairway driver goes as far as the distance driver, but straighter | Speed is not your limit; form is | Keep the fairway driver and work on form |
| An understable disc turns right and keeps going right | Your arm speed is higher than that disc needs, or you are releasing with the nose up or off-axis | Try a more stable disc, then check your release on video |
| Every disc flies a different line each throw | The release is the variable, not the disc | Fix consistency before buying more discs |

That last row matters more than any number. If your throws scatter, a new disc will not fix it. Film a few throws and look at your release. See [how to film your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis) for a setup that works with one phone.

### When to move up in speed

Move up when your current fastest disc starts behaving more understable than its numbers suggest on a clean, flat throw, and the next speed up still shows some turn instead of fading straight away. Arm speed comes from the body, not the disc, so the shortest path to throwing faster discs is usually better form. [How to throw farther in disc golf](/blog/how-to-throw-farther-disc-golf) covers the mechanics side of that.

## A beginner bag of 3 to 5 discs

You do not need a full bag to start. A small set teaches you more because you learn what each disc does.

1. **Putter** (speed 2 to 3, low turn, low fade): putting and short approaches.
2. **Midrange** (speed 4 to 5, near-zero turn): straight shots under about the distance you comfortably reach, and the best disc to learn form with.
3. **Understable fairway driver** (speed 6 to 7, turn around -2): your main driver while your arm speed builds.
4. **Optional: stable or overstable midrange or approach disc** (fade 2 or more): reliable fade for wind and for shots that need to finish left.
5. **Optional: understable distance driver** (speed 9 to 10, negative turn, lighter weight): only when the fairway driver feels easy.

## Disc legality basics (PDGA technical standards)

If you play sanctioned events, your discs need to be on the PDGA Approved Discs list. The [PDGA technical standards](https://www.pdga.com/technical-standards/guidelines) set the physical limits a disc must meet to be approved, including:

- a maximum weight of 200 grams, and no more than 8.3 grams per centimeter of outside diameter
- a diameter between 21 and 30 centimeters
- a rim width of no more than 2.6 centimeters
- a flexibility limit of 27 pounds

None of these limits mention flight numbers. A legal disc can carry any numbers its maker chooses, which is one more reason to test discs yourself.

## How flight numbers fit with form work

Flight numbers assume a clean, flat release at the disc's rated speed. Most strange flights come from the throw: a nose-up release, wobble from off-axis torque, or an early release that sends the disc right of your line. Those show up on video long before they show up in a new disc.

When a disc keeps doing something its numbers say it should not, film it and compare a good throw with a bad one. A [3D form analysis](/features/3d-throw) of both throws, side by side and synced at release, makes the difference easier to see than a single 2D clip, because you can turn the view to top-down or behind. ## Quick reference

- **Speed** is the power a disc needs. Match it to your arm.
- **Glide** is float.
- **Turn** is the early right drift on RHBH. More negative means more drift.
- **Fade** is the late left hook. Higher means harder.
- **Compare** turn and fade only within the same speed, and only loosely across brands.
- **Test** in a field. Your throws are the real flight chart.
