<Answer>

To read your disc golf throw in 3D, work through four views and five phases. Start top-down to check the pull path and hip-shoulder turn, then behind for the power pocket, side for the plant and brace, and front for when your chest opens. In each view, step through the reach-back, plant, pocket, release and follow-through at 1/4 speed, using the release marker as your anchor and the hand trail to see the pull. Pick one fault to work on, not five.

</Answer>

A 3D replay of your throw is a lot of information at once. You can spin it anywhere, slow it down and scrub through every frame, and the first few times most people just rotate it around and say "huh, cool." This guide gives you a routine so you get something useful out of it every time.

Full disclosure: I build Disc Golf Form Analyzer, and its 3D Throw feature is the viewer I have in mind here (views, 1/4 and 1/2 speed, a release marker and a hand trail). The checklist works with any 3D replay that has similar controls. If you are new to the idea, start with [what disc golf 3D form analysis is](/blog/disc-golf-3d-form-analysis).

## Before you read your throw in 3D: set up the replay

A few habits make every review faster.

1. **Watch once at full speed.** Do not touch anything. Just get the overall rhythm of the throw.
2. **Find the release marker.** Scrub to the moment the disc leaves your hand. That frame is your anchor. Every other check is "before release" or "after release."
3. **Turn on the hand trail.** It draws where your throwing hand went, which makes the pull path visible in every view.
4. **Switch to 1/4 speed** for the phase checks. Use frame-by-frame scrubbing for the moments that matter.
5. **Go back to full speed before you judge timing.** Slow motion stretches everything. A move that is late at full speed can look fine at 1/4. A forum thread on [filming drives for critique](https://www.dgcoursereview.com/threads/best-angle-for-filming-drives-for-critique.40170/) makes the same point about slow-motion video: use it for positions, judge timing at full speed.

One honest note before the checklist. A 3D replay built from a single phone video estimates depth, and the axis pointing at your original camera is the least certain one. The fastest moment (the hand at release) is also the blurriest in the source video. So trust big, clear shapes and treat tiny differences as noise.

## The five phases

These are the moments to stop on. Names vary between coaches; the positions do not.

| Phase | Where it is | What it should look like (backhand) |
|---|---|---|
| Reach-back | The disc at its furthest point back | Disc back along the target line, shoulders turned away, weight not drifting backward |
| Plant | The front foot lands | Front foot down before the arm starts forward, body still closed |
| Pocket | The disc passes the chest | Disc close to the chest, elbow bent and leading toward the target |
| Release | The release marker | Arm extending, front leg firm, hand close to your body line |
| Follow-through | After the disc leaves | Body rotates through and stops balanced over the front foot |

For forehand, the phases are similar, but you check a compact elbow and the forearm and wrist leading the disc instead of a chest pocket. [Disc golf forehand basics](/blog/disc-golf-forehand-basics) covers what those positions should be.

## View 1: top-down (start here)

Top-down is the view your phone could never give you, and it shows the most about the backhand.

**Reach-back**
- Is the disc back along the target line, or swung out to the side, away from your body?
- Are your shoulders turned well past your hips, or is the chest still facing mostly forward? If the arm went back but the shoulders did not, that is an arm swing, not a coil. See [disc golf reach-back](/blog/disc-golf-reach-back).

**Plant to pocket**
- Picture your hip line and shoulder line as two bars. Does the hip bar start turning toward the target before the shoulder bar? That gap is the hip lead coaches talk about. If both bars turn together, the body is moving as one block.

**Pocket to release**
- Look at the hand trail. A clean pull draws a fairly straight line forward along the target line, passing close to your chest. Rounding draws a wide arc out past the shoulder. If you see an arc, [how to stop rounding your backhand](/blog/how-to-stop-rounding-disc-golf-backhand) has the causes and drills.

**Follow-through**
- Where does the arm finish relative to the target line? A finish that wraps far around can be fine; a release that already happened far to the side is the real issue.

A caveat for top-down specifically: if you filmed side-on, the sideways direction in the top-down view is the depth your camera had to estimate. A clear arc is still a clear arc. A borderline one is worth confirming with a clip filmed from behind.

## View 2: behind (down the target line)

This is the classic angle for rounding and the power pocket, now available even if you filmed from the side.

**Reach-back**
- Is your head roughly steady, or does it drift back away from the target as you reach? Sway in the reach-back often turns into a lunge forward later.

**Pocket**
- At the pocket frame, how far is the disc from your chest? Is the elbow out in front leading the motion, or trailing behind the hand?

**Release**
- Scrub to the release marker. Is the hand close to your body line, or already well out to the side? The second usually means the disc left on an arc.

**Follow-through**
- Does the body rotate around a stable front side, or does it tip away?

## View 3: side

Side-on shows the stride, the plant and the brace, and it is the angle most people film anyway.

**Reach-back to plant**
- Where is your weight at full reach-back? A big lean back toward the rear foot means you will have to lurch forward.
- When does the front foot land relative to the arm? On a well-timed throw, the front foot is down before the arm starts its forward pull. If the arm is already moving forward while the front foot is still in the air, plant timing is a likely issue.

**Plant to release**
- Watch the front leg. Does it firm up and stop the body's forward drift (a brace), or keep bending and let the body slide past it? [Hips, brace and weight shift](/blog/disc-golf-hips-brace-weight-shift) explains why that front-side stop matters.
- Check your posture. Is there a consistent athletic bend at the hips, or does the upper body stand straight up or fold over during the throw?

**Follow-through**
- Do you finish over the front foot, or does the back foot have to step through to catch you? Occasional is normal on a full-power drive; every time suggests the weight shift is off.

## View 4: front

The front view shows when your chest opens toward the target, which is hard to see from anywhere else.

**Plant**
- At the plant frame, which way is your chest facing? If it is already square to the target, you have opened up early, and the hips and shoulders have little left to give. That is the "early rotation (opening up)" fault, and it is often what someone means when they say a throw is all arm.

**Pocket to release**
- From the front you can see how far the elbow sits from your body. For forehand, this is the angle for the chicken wing (the elbow flaring away from the body).

**Finish**
- Are your head and hips stacked over the front foot, or falling to one side?

## Putting the checklist together

Here is the whole routine in one list. It takes about five minutes per throw once you know it.

1. Full speed, default view, no pausing.
2. Find and note the release marker.
3. Turn on the hand trail.
4. Top-down at 1/4 speed: reach-back line, shoulder turn, hip lead, pull path.
5. Behind: head sway, pocket distance, hand position at release.
6. Side: weight at reach-back, plant timing, brace, posture, finish.
7. Front: chest direction at plant, elbow distance, finish balance.
8. Back to full speed to sanity-check timing.
9. Write down the single biggest issue you saw.
10. Pick one drill for that issue and film again after a practice block.

## Common mistakes when reading a 3D replay

**Fixing everything at once.** A 3D view will show you five things you do not love. Pick the one that comes earliest in the throw, because early faults often cause later ones. A wide reach-back often leads to a rounded pull, so fix the reach-back first.

**Over-reading small differences.** A few degrees here or there is within what a single camera can estimate. Look for differences you could describe to a friend in one sentence.

**Judging the release frame too precisely.** The hand at release is the fastest, blurriest part of the original video. Use the release marker as a reference point for body positions (front leg, hips, elbow), not as a measurement of the wrist to the millimeter.

**Reviewing after every throw.** The guidance hypothesis in motor learning says feedback can hurt learning if it comes too often or is too easy to lean on ([Anderson et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1780106/)). Throw a set, then review the set. If you build several throws in a session, compare them instead of analyzing each one to death.

**Thinking about body parts while you throw.** Use the 3D review to find the issue, then practice with a cue about the result rather than the body. Research on attention generally favors an external focus, the effect of the movement, over an internal one, the body movement itself ([Wulf 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2012.723728)). "Pull the disc along the rope" usually beats "keep my elbow in."

## When one replay is not enough

The most useful thing after reading one throw is reading two. Put a good throw next to a bad one, line them up at release, and the difference you were hunting for often becomes obvious.
