<Answer>

A good disc golf forehand (also called a sidearm) is a body throw that ends in a short, fast forearm and wrist snap, not an arm flick. Grip the disc with your middle finger against the inside of the rim and your thumb on top, keep your elbow close to your side and leading, let your hips turn first, and keep the disc flat or slightly nose down. Forehand wobble usually means too much arm and too little spin, or a wrist that rolls over at release. Fix the sequence first, and the spin and wobble often fix themselves.

</Answer>

The forehand is the throw most players pick up second, and it often goes one of two ways: it works right away for short shots, or it wobbles, flips and hurts. Either way, it usually stalls as soon as you try to throw it hard, because the arm takes over. This guide covers grip options, the mechanics step by step, why forehand wobble happens, how to check yours on video, and drills.

Full disclosure: I build Disc Golf Form Analyzer, a phone app that grades throwing form from video, including the forehand. I will point out where it helps.

## Forehand grip options

There is no single correct grip, but most players use one of these. They are described for a right-hand forehand.

| Grip | Fingers | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Two-finger split | Middle finger pad against the inside rim, index finger spread toward the center of the disc | Control and approaches |
| Two-finger stacked (power) | Middle finger against the rim, index finger stacked on top of it for support | Drives and extra snap |
| One-finger | Only the middle finger on the rim, index finger off it | Short shots, players with small hands |

In all of them, the thumb presses on top of the flight plate, near the rim, and the ring and little fingers tuck out of the way. The rim should sit firmly in the crook between your thumb and index finger.

Grip details matter more than they seem. A 2024 study on backhand grip, using 24 players, 600 throws and a sensor-fitted mid-range disc, found that thumb position changed both spin and launch speed, with a strong link between spin rate and launch speed across throws ([Koch et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/10/241022153937.htm)). That was a backhand study, but the lesson carries: small changes in where your fingers sit change what the disc gets. Experiment one variable at a time.

## Disc golf forehand mechanics, step by step

Forehands come in many styles, but strong ones share the same order: legs and hips first, then the torso, then the elbow, forearm and wrist. That proximal-to-distal order is the standard model for fast throws in biomechanics ([Putnam 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(93)90084-R)).

| Phase | What to do | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Setup | Stand roughly facing the target, slightly open, disc at waist to chest height | Starting with the disc far out to the side |
| 2. Load | Shift weight onto the throwing-side (right) leg and turn the hips and shoulders back slightly | Staying upright with no load on the back leg |
| 3. Elbow leads | Keep the elbow close to your side and bent, leading the disc forward | The elbow flying out away from the body |
| 4. Hips turn | Let the hips start rotating toward the target while the disc and hand trail behind | The arm starting before the hips |
| 5. Lag | The disc trails the hand slightly, with the wrist cocked back | Pushing the disc forward with no lag |
| 6. Snap | The forearm and wrist whip through, palm turning down, and the disc leaves flat | A wrist that rolls over or a release with the disc tilted |
| 7. Finish | Weight moves onto the front (left) leg; balanced follow-through | Falling away or spinning off balance |

The snap is where the spin comes from, and there is research on it. In a 2008 study of ultimate players throwing sidearm for distance, skilled throwers released the disc at a similar speed to unskilled throwers but spun it faster and threw much farther. The key difference was the forearm in the last acceleration phase: skilled throwers pronated it (rotated it palm-down), while unskilled throwers supinated it ([Sasakawa and Sakurai 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18972880/)). It was an ultimate study, not disc golf, but the motion is close, and it fits the coaching cue of a palm-down snap.

## Spin, wobble and off-axis torque on forehand

A follow-up study of forehand throws found that throwing distance correlated with spin velocity in both skilled throwers (r = 0.722) and unskilled throwers (r = 0.794) ([Sasakawa, Umegaki and Sakurai 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02640414.2017.1344778)). More spin went with longer throws. That is the most useful thing to know about forehand wobble: a wobbling disc is usually a disc with a lot of arm speed and not enough spin, or one that was twisted out of its plane as it left.

Wobble is the visible sign of off-axis torque, a twist on the disc that is not around its spin axis. A disc with off-axis torque flutters for a moment before it settles ([TechDisc metrics](https://techdisc.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/153000074253-techdisc-metrics)). The usual forehand causes, from coaching consensus:

- **Arm-only throwing.** No hip turn, so the arm muscles the disc and there is no lag or snap.
- **The wrist rolling over.** The hand turns so the thumb side drops at release, which twists the disc.
- **Elbow escape (chicken wing).** The elbow lifts and flies away from the body, so the forearm swings on a wide, unstable path. We cover it in [how to fix a forehand chicken wing](/blog/fix-forehand-chicken-wing).
- **A mismatched release angle.** The disc leaves at a different angle from the one your arm traveled on.

For more on wobble, nose angle and off-axis torque in general, see [nose up, off-axis torque and early release](/blog/disc-golf-nose-up-off-axis-torque-early-release).

## Elbow-friendly mechanics

A 2015 online survey of 883 disc golfers found that 86.2% threw mainly backhand and 12.7% mainly forehand, and that forehand throwers were more likely to report an elbow injury (P = .014) ([Nelson et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4622370/)). It was a self-selected survey, so it does not tell you how common injuries are or what caused them, and nothing here is medical advice. If your elbow hurts, see a medical professional.

What coaches generally agree on is that a forehand that leans on the body is less of a strain on the arm than one that tries to generate everything from the elbow and wrist. In practice:

- Let the hips and torso start the throw, so the arm is not trying to create speed on its own.
- Keep the elbow close to your side and bent, not locked out or flying away.
- Keep the snap short and fast at the end, not a long, forced flick.
- Build power gradually; do not start your sessions with full-power forehands.

## How to spot forehand faults on video

Film from the front or from behind for the elbow and wrist, and side-on for the load, sequence and weight shift. Our guide to [filming your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis) covers the setup. Pause on these frames:

1. **The load.** Is your weight on the back leg, with the hips turned back a little?
2. **The start of the forward move.** Do the hips move before the hand? If the hand moves first, the throw is arm-led.
3. **Mid-pull.** Is the elbow close to your side, or has it lifted and moved away (front or behind view)?
4. **Just before release.** Is the disc trailing the hand (lag), or is the hand pushing it?
5. **The first frames after release.** A visible flutter means off-axis torque.

## Forehand drills

Start every drill at low power with a putter or mid-range. Spin first, distance later.

### Flat snap reps

From about 30 feet, throw a putter flat at a target with only a short arm motion. The goal is a quiet, spinning disc with no wobble. If it wobbles, slow down and focus on a firm wrist and a palm-down snap.

### Elbow-at-the-hip throws

Throw short forehands while keeping your elbow brushing your side through the pull. If it lifts, the throw is too hard. This teaches the compact path.

### Hips-first forehands

Load onto the back leg, then start the throw with only a hip turn and count "one" before the arm moves. Throw easy. The point is to feel the arm being pulled by the body.

### Spin ladder

Throw ten easy forehands and count how many leave without wobble. Add a little pace, throw ten more, and count again. Stop adding pace at the speed where wobble comes back. That is your current clean forehand, and the number goes up as the mechanics improve.

### Best versus typical comparison

Film your cleanest forehand of the day and a typical one from the same angle. Compare them at the moment of release: elbow position, wrist angle, and where your weight is. With 3D Throw you can put them side by side, synced at release.
