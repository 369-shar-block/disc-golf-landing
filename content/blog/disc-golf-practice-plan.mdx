<Answer>

A good disc golf practice plan uses short sessions with one focus each, a weekly mix of field work, putting, a drill for your main fault and at least one round, and video reviewed in batches instead of after every throw. Cue the flight of the disc or a target rather than your body parts once a drill feels comfortable. Motor-learning research supports all three habits: constant feedback can make you dependent on it, an external focus generally helps learning more than an internal one, and mixing up your practice tends to help skills stick.

</Answer>

Most players practice by playing. That is fun and it helps scoring, but a round gives you maybe a dozen drives, each from a different tee with a different shot shape. If you are trying to change your form, that is not enough reps of the same movement to learn anything. This guide gives you a plan that fixes that, and it is built around how people actually learn motor skills.

Full disclosure: I build Disc Golf Form Analyzer, so I mention where video review fits. The plan works with a phone camera and no app at all.

## The principles behind the plan

### 1. Batch your feedback

The guidance hypothesis in motor learning holds that augmented feedback can hurt learning when it is provided too often. In one test of the idea, learners who got feedback delayed by two trials performed a bit worse during practice but declined much less once feedback was taken away, and they reported using more of their own internal feedback ([Anderson et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1780106/)).

For disc golf, that means: do not walk to your phone after every throw. Throw a set, then watch the set.

### 2. Focus on the effect, not the body part

A long line of research finds that an external focus (on the effect of your movement) generally improves performance and learning compared with an internal focus (on the body movement itself) ([Wulf 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2012.723728)). Not every reviewer agrees on how strong the effect is, but it is a cheap habit to try. Use body cues to learn a drill, then switch to an outside cue: the disc's line, a gap between trees, a spot on the ground.

### 3. Mix up your practice once the movement is stable

Blocked practice, the same throw over and over, builds a new movement quickly. Random or varied practice, changing disc, distance or shot between throws, tends to improve retention and transfer, an idea that goes back to the contextual interference study by [Shea and Morgan 1979](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.5.2.179). The effect has not held up in every sport study, so the practical rule is simple: block while you change form, vary once it holds.

### 4. Pair video with a specific cue

Video works best with something specific to look for. In an overarm throwing study, learners whose feedback came with attention-focusing cues or error-correcting information did better than those told only their results ([Kernodle and Carlton 1992](https://doi.org/10.1080/00222895.1992.9941614)). So never just "watch your form." Watch for one thing.

## A weekly disc golf practice plan

Here is a template for a player with about four to five hours a week. Scale the session lengths up or down, but keep the structure.

| Day | Session | Length | Focus | Video |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Rest or mobility | 15 min | Easy movement | None |
| Tue | Drill session | 30–40 min | One drill for your main fault, blocked reps at low power | Film one set near the end; review after |
| Wed | Putting | 20–30 min | Blocked reps, then varied distances | Film one set of 10 putts every other week |
| Thu | Field work | 45–60 min | Same fault, now at fuller power, mixed discs | Film 3–5 throws per disc; review as a batch |
| Fri | Putting | 20 min | Pressure routines | None |
| Sat | Round | 1.5–2 h | Score; play the shots, don't think about mechanics | None |
| Sun | Field work or casual round | 45–60 min | Varied shots: hyzer, flat, anhyzer, forehand | Film a "check" set every two weeks |

Why this shape works:

- **Form work comes before power.** Tuesday's drill session is slow and blocked. Thursday's field work tests the same change at fuller effort and with different discs.
- **Rounds are for playing.** On Saturday, trust what you have. Mid-round swing thoughts rarely help, and the round tells you whether practice is transferring.
- **Video is regular but not constant.** You film in most form sessions, review in batches and do a bigger comparison every two weeks.

## How to run a field work session

Field work is throwing a stack of discs in an open field, walking out and throwing back. Done with a plan, it is the most productive practice in disc golf.

1. **Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes.** Easy throws with a putter or midrange at short distance.
2. **Pick one focus.** One fault from your last video review, or one shot you want to own. Write it down.
3. **Throw a blocked set.** Five to ten throws of the same disc and shot at about 70 percent effort, thinking about the focus.
4. **Walk and look.** Before you pick discs up, look at the landing pattern. Tight group or scatter? Left or right of your line?
5. **Film one set.** Phone set up side-on or behind, depending on the fault. Do not check it yet.
6. **Throw a varied set.** Change discs or shot shape each throw, with your focus turned into an outside cue.
7. **Review the video at the end.** Watch the filmed set in one sitting. Pick the one thing to change next session.

For where to put the phone and which angle shows which fault, see [how to film your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis).

## How often to film and review

A cadence that respects the guidance hypothesis looks like this:

- **Every form session:** film one set of 3 to 5 throws near the end. Review it after the session, not between throws.
- **Every two weeks:** film a "check" set from the same camera spot, same height and same disc. Compare it with the check set from two weeks earlier.
- **When you change something big** (new footwork, new grip): film before and after, so you have a baseline.
- **Never mid-round in competition.** Save it for practice.

Comparing check sets is where a 3D view helps. Two clips filmed on different days rarely line up perfectly, but if you rebuild both as 3D bodies you can view them side by side, synced at release, from any angle, including top-down. ## Don't practice mid-round in tournaments

Under PDGA rule 809.03, a practice throw during a round, meaning any throw that is not a competitive attempt to improve your lie, costs one penalty throw ([PDGA 809.03](https://www.pdga.com/rules/official-rules-disc-golf/80903)). There are narrow exceptions, such as moving an unused disc a short distance. That is one more reason to keep form work for practice days and treat the round as a test.

In casual rounds it is up to your group, but the same logic applies: a round with swing thoughts on every tee is not great practice and not great golf.

## Adjusting the plan

- **Less time?** Keep one drill session, one field work session and one putting session. Drop the second putting day first.
- **More time?** Add a second field work session with a different focus, such as forehand or approach shots.
- **Plateaued?** Film a check set and look for one fault that shows up on most throws. Make that the only focus for two weeks.
- **Sore?** Cut volume. More throws at full effort is not the goal; more good reps is.

The plan works because it is boring in the right way: one focus, enough reps, feedback in batches and a regular check on whether anything changed.
