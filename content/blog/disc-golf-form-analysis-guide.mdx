<Answer>

Disc golf form analysis means studying how your body moves through a throw to find the one fault that limits you most, then fixing it with a targeted drill. The core method is the same whatever tool you use: film a few throws side-on and from behind, review them at full speed and in slow motion, find the earliest clear fault, practice one drill, and film again. You can do the diagnosis yourself, post a form check, book a coach, or use vision software and 3D replays to track your body automatically.

</Answer>

## What form analysis is (and is not)

Form analysis looks at your body: the reach-back, the plant, the hips, the brace, the pull, the release and the finish. It is not the same as measuring the disc. A sensor disc tells you how fast the disc left your hand, how much it spun and at what angle. Form analysis tells you why your body produced those numbers.

Good analysis ends with three things:

1. **One main issue.** Not a list of twelve.
2. **A reason.** Why that issue costs you distance or accuracy.
3. **A drill.** Something specific to practice, and a way to check whether it worked.

## Your options for disc golf form analysis

| Option | What it is | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-review video | You film and watch your own throws | Free, fast, builds your eye | Hard to know what "right" looks like; easy to miss the cause |
| Form check posts | You post clips to forums or communities | Many eyes, free | Mixed advice quality, conflicting opinions, depends on your filming |
| Coach lesson | A coach watches in person or reviews your video | Expert judgment, can test fixes live | Costs money, depends on availability and the coach |
| Sensor disc | A disc with sensors records its own flight data | Precise disc numbers (speed, spin, angles) | Does not see your body; needs the hardware |
| Vision software | An app tracks your body from video and grades it | Fast, repeatable, available anytime | Less precise than a lab; quality depends on filming |
| 3D replay | Your video rebuilt as a 3D body you can rotate | Shows views you did not film, like top-down | One camera still estimates depth; no disc data |

These are not rivals so much as layers. Many players self-review, post a form check when stuck, and use software to check between sessions.

### Self-review video

Start here no matter what else you use. Filming yourself builds the skill of seeing your own throw, and that skill makes every other option more useful. The weakness is that you do not always know what you are looking at, which is where a checklist helps. [Disc golf form check](/blog/disc-golf-form-check) has one.

### Form check posts

Posting clips gets you free feedback from players who have seen thousands of throws. The quality varies, and threads often argue. You get far better answers when your clip is well filmed and you tell people what disc you threw and how it flew. [Disc golf form check](/blog/disc-golf-form-check) covers how to ask, and how to judge the answers.

### Coach lessons

A good coach does something no tool does: they can watch you try a fix, adjust it on the spot and tell you how it should feel. If you can get regular lessons, they are hard to beat. Video between lessons helps the coach see your habits, not just your best day.

### Sensor discs

A sensor disc records the disc's own numbers. TechDisc, for example, reports speed, spin, nose angle, launch angle, hyzer angle and wobble ([TechDisc metrics](https://techdisc.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/153000074253-techdisc-metrics)). That is ideal for questions like "am I releasing nose up?" which a phone camera cannot measure precisely. It does not tell you what your body did to cause it.

### Vision software

Pose estimation software finds your joints in every frame of a video, with no markers or sensors, and compares your positions and timing to a reference. Accuracy is good for clear shapes and short of a lab for small angles: a multi-phone research system, OpenCap, measured joint angles with a 4.5 degree mean absolute error against lab motion capture ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1011462)), and a single phone is less precise than that. The details are in [how disc golf form analysis software works](/blog/how-disc-golf-form-analysis-software-works).

### 3D replays

3D body reconstruction turns one phone video into a 3D figure you can rotate. The big win is views you never filmed, especially top-down, which shows the pull path and hip-shoulder turn directly. A single camera still has to estimate depth. See [disc golf 3D form analysis](/blog/disc-golf-3d-form-analysis) for what 3D shows and where it falls short.

## How to run a form analysis session

This is the routine that works, whichever option you use for the diagnosis.

### Step 1: Film well

Bad filming is the biggest cause of bad analysis. The short version:

1. Two angles: side-on and directly behind.
2. Phone at about hip height, level, in landscape, roughly 15 to 25 feet away.
3. Whole body in frame from reach-back to follow-through, plant foot included.
4. Slow motion in bright, even light.
5. Three or four throws with the same setup.

The full guide is [how to film your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis).

### Step 2: Watch at full speed first

Get the overall rhythm before you pause anything. Slow motion makes timing look smoother than it is.

### Step 3: Stop on the key positions

Step through each throw and pause on the same five moments:

1. End of the reach-back
2. The plant (front foot lands)
3. The pocket (disc passes the chest)
4. Release
5. Finish

On each one, ask the same questions every time. For a backhand: Is the disc back along the target line? Is the front foot down before the arm pulls? Is the disc close to the chest with the elbow leading? Is the front leg firm at release? Do you finish balanced over the front foot?

### Step 4: Compare

A good throw next to a bad one, lined up at release, often shows the fault faster than any amount of staring at one clip. [Compare disc golf throws side by side](/blog/compare-disc-golf-throws-side-by-side) explains how.

### Step 5: Name one fault

Write it down in one sentence. "My front leg keeps bending after the plant instead of firming up." If you cannot say it in one sentence, keep looking or ask for help.

### Step 6: Pick one drill and practice in blocks

Practice the drill at low power until the shape holds, then build speed. Review video at the end of a block, not after every throw. The guidance hypothesis in motor learning holds that feedback can hurt learning when it comes too often or is too easy to lean on ([Anderson et al. 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1780106/)).

### Step 7: Film again, same setup

Same spot, same angle, same light if you can. Compare the new throw with the old one. If the position you worked on changed, move to the next issue.

## What to fix first

The throw is a chain. Classic biomechanics describes throwing as proximal-to-distal: larger segments near the body's center start the motion, and the arm and hand finish it ([Putnam 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(93)90084-R)). In practice that means faults early in the throw often cause the ones later in it.

A useful order of priority:

1. **Posture and balance.** If you cannot stay in a stable athletic position, nothing above it is reliable.
2. **The plant and the brace.** A late plant or a front leg that collapses takes away what the hips turn against. See [hips, brace and weight shift](/blog/disc-golf-hips-brace-weight-shift).
3. **The reach-back and coil.** A wide reach-back without a shoulder turn often turns into a rounded pull. See [disc golf reach-back](/blog/disc-golf-reach-back).
4. **Opening up early.** If your chest faces the target at the plant, the arm has to do all the work. See [early rotation and opening up](/blog/disc-golf-early-rotation-opening-up).
5. **The pull path and pocket.** Rounding and losing the power pocket. See [how to stop rounding your backhand](/blog/how-to-stop-rounding-disc-golf-backhand).
6. **The release.** Nose angle, early release and off-axis torque. See [nose up, off-axis torque and early release](/blog/disc-golf-nose-up-off-axis-torque-early-release).

This is a rule of thumb, not a law. If one fault is obviously the biggest, fix that one. But when you see several problems at once, start with the earliest.

## What phone video can and cannot tell you

Phone video, reviewed by eye or by software, is strong for:

- Sequencing: what moves first, hips or arm
- Plant timing and weight shift
- Posture and the brace
- The pull path (from behind, or top-down in 3D)
- Balance at the finish

It is weak for:

- **Nose angle.** A small tilt of a thin, fast object. Use flight and field work, or a sensor disc.
- **Exact speeds.** Frames are too far apart at the hit to measure hand speed precisely.
- **Tiny angle differences.** A few degrees is within what a single camera can estimate.
- **Hidden body parts.** When your arm passes behind your body, the camera cannot see it.

A review of 2D video analysis in sport notes that images need to be "sharp and motion blur-free, especially in high speed motions" for accurate analysis ([Pueo 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3010/301049620005.pdf)). Bright light and a higher frame rate are the cheapest upgrades you can make.

## Mistakes that waste a form analysis

**Analyzing one throw.** One throw can be a fluke. Film several and look for what repeats.

**Fixing five things.** One fault at a time. The others often improve along with it.

**Starting with the arm.** The arm is where faults show up, not always where they start.

**Copying a pro exactly.** Copy order and relationships (hips before shoulders, disc close to the chest), not exact angles. Your body is not theirs.

**Reviewing after every throw.** Batch your feedback.

**Thinking about body parts while throwing.** Find the fault on video, then practice with a cue about the result. Research on attention generally favors focusing on the effect of a movement over the movement itself ([Wulf 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2012.723728)).

## Your form analysis checklist

1. Film three or four throws, side-on and from behind, whole body in frame, good light.
2. Watch at full speed, then step through reach-back, plant, pocket, release and finish.
3. Compare a good throw with a bad one, synced at release.
4. Name the earliest clear fault in one sentence.
5. Pick one drill and practice it at low power in blocks.
6. Review in batches, not after every throw.
7. Film again with the same setup and compare with the old throw.
8. When the position holds, move on to the next fault.

Do this a few times and something else happens: you start seeing your own throw clearly, without a camera. That is the real goal of form analysis.
