<Answer>

In a good backhand, power is built from the ground up in order: your weight moves onto the front leg, the front leg plants and braces, the disc golf hips turn toward the target while the shoulders stay coiled, and then the torso, arm and wrist fire one after another. This order is called the kinetic chain. Most "all arm" throws are a chain that fires out of order (shoulders before hips) or a front leg that never stops the body (a weak brace). Film side-on to check the brace and weight shift, and front-on to check whether your hips lead.

</Answer>

"Use your hips" and "brace your front leg" are probably the two most common pieces of advice in disc golf form threads, and also two of the most confusing. Both are true, but they only make sense as parts of one sequence. This guide walks through that sequence link by link, shows what each link looks like on video, and gives you a drill for the usual breakdowns.

Full disclosure: I build Disc Golf Form Analyzer, a phone app that grades throwing form from video. Several of the faults below are ones the app grades, and I will say which.

## The disc golf kinetic chain, in order

Throwing biomechanics has a standard model for how fast throws work: proximal-to-distal sequencing. The big, slow segments near the center of the body start the motion, and each faster, smaller segment further out peaks after the one before it ([Putnam 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(93)90084-R)). Think of cracking a whip: a modest move at the handle produces a very fast tip, but only if the motion travels down the whip in order.

For a backhand, the chain looks like this. Directions are for a right-hand backhand.

| Link | What it does | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Back leg | Loads in the reach-back and pushes you toward the target | Staying tall and passive on the back leg |
| 2. Front leg plant | Lands and starts to take your weight | Landing late, after the arm has started |
| 3. The brace | Front leg firms up and stops forward movement | Knee collapsing forward or the body sliding past the foot |
| 4. Hips | Turn toward the target while the shoulders stay back | Hips and shoulders turning together, or shoulders first |
| 5. Torso and shoulders | Unwind fast, pulling the arm through | Opening the chest before the front foot lands |
| 6. Arm and elbow | Pull the disc close past the chest, then extend | The arm swinging wide (rounding) |
| 7. Wrist and disc | Snap the disc out at the hit | Releasing early or late |

Each link hands its speed to the next. When one fires early, it has nothing behind it to push against, and the next link has to make up the difference with muscle.

## Disc golf hips: what "hips lead" looks like on video

"Hips lead" does not mean the hips spin hard on their own. It means the hips start turning toward the target slightly before the shoulders do, so for a brief moment your belt buckle points more toward the target than your chest. That twist between hips and shoulders, often called hip-shoulder separation, stretches the torso like a rubber band, and the shoulders then snap around to catch up.

On video, from the front or slightly off the front:

- **At the moment the front foot lands,** your chest should still be turned mostly away from the target, with the disc still back.
- **Just after the plant,** your hips should be visibly more open toward the target than your shoulders.
- **The fault** is the reverse: the chest already facing the target while the front foot is still in the air. That is opening up early, and it usually sends the arm around the body.

In Pose Estimation, the app grades two versions of this: "Early rotation (opening up)" and "Early upper body rotation." Both are visible from front or side video.

## The disc golf brace: what the research says

The brace is the front leg planting and firming so it stops your forward motion. Once the lower body stops, the upper body keeps going and whips around the front leg. A soft front knee that keeps bending or sliding forward means the body never stops, so the whip never cracks.

There is not much disc golf research on this, but there is some. A 2025 bachelor's thesis at the University of Jyvaskyla used force plates with 10 male disc golfers, from hobby players to international level, and looked at their fastest backhand ([Tervonen 2025](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/102903)). Larger peak braking forces under the front leg went with higher launch speed (correlations around 0.73-0.75 in size), and peak lead-knee extension velocity was associated with launch speed (r = 0.781) when the follow-through was included. The author is careful: the timing of the knee peaks varied a lot between throwers, so its exact role is uncertain. It is a small thesis, not a published study. Still, it points the same way coaches do: a front leg that pushes back against the ground, rather than giving way, goes with a faster disc.

On video, side-on:

- **Good:** the front knee lands slightly bent and then holds or straightens as you throw. Your head and hips stop moving forward over the front foot.
- **Fault:** the front knee keeps bending forward past the toes, or your whole body slides forward past the plant.

The app grades this as "Weak front-side brace," visible from side or front video.

## Weight shift: back leg to front leg

Weight shift is the slower, larger move that the brace finishes. In the reach-back, your weight sits mostly on the back leg. As the front foot lands, your weight moves toward it, and by the release you should be mostly on the front leg, finishing balanced over it.

Two opposite faults break it:

1. **Staying back.** Weight never really gets to the front foot. You throw "falling away," often with the disc climbing nose up, because your shoulders tilt back.
2. **Lunging through.** Weight rushes forward past the front foot, the brace never holds, and you stumble out of the throw.

Side-on video shows both clearly. Look at where your head and hips are relative to your front foot at release. The app grades this as "Incomplete weight transfer / rotation" from side or behind video, and checks timing as "Poor plant timing."

## How to spot a broken chain on video

Film two angles if you can: side-on for the brace, plant and weight shift, and front-on for hip lead. Our guide to [filming your disc golf throw for form analysis](/blog/how-to-film-disc-golf-throw-for-form-analysis) has the setup. Then pause on three frames:

1. **Front foot first touches the ground.** Is the disc still back? Is the chest still turned away? If the arm has already started forward, the plant is late.
2. **Just after the plant.** Are the hips more open than the shoulders? Is the front knee holding?
3. **Release.** Is your weight over the front foot, with your head steady above it?

A flat video makes hip and shoulder angles hard to judge, because they turn toward and away from the camera. That is where [3D form analysis](/features/3d-throw) helps: rebuild the throw as a 3D body, switch to the top-down view and you can see the hip line and shoulder line separate as you turn. Slow it to 1/4 speed around the plant, and compare a good throw and a bad one side by side, synced at release, to see which link changed.

## Common breakdowns and drills

Match the drill to what your video shows. Start at half power; the chain is about timing, and timing is easiest to learn slowly.

| What the video shows | Fault name in the app | Best angle | Drill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest faces the target before the front foot lands | Early rotation (opening up) | Front, side | Closed-shoulder plant |
| Arm moves before the hips | Early upper body rotation | Front, side | Hips-first standstill |
| Front knee keeps bending forward | Weak front-side brace | Side, front | Brace and hold |
| Arm already moving when the foot lands | Poor plant timing | Side, behind | Step and pause |
| Weight stays back or lunges past the foot | Incomplete weight transfer / rotation | Side, behind | Weight-shift walk-through |
| Stumbling out of the throw | Poor finish and balance | Any | Brace and hold |

### Hips-first standstill

Stand in your throwing stance with the disc already in the reach-back. Start the forward move with only a hip turn and a push from the back leg, and say "one" before the arm is allowed to move. Throw gently. The point is to make a late arm feel normal.

### Closed-shoulder plant

Make slow throws where you deliberately keep your chest turned away from the target until you feel the front foot land. A good external cue: keep your back toward the target until your foot hits the ground.

### Brace and hold

From a standstill, plant and throw at about 70% effort, then freeze on your front leg for two seconds after release. If you cannot hold the finish, or your front knee drifts forward past your toes, the brace is soft. Repeat until the hold feels easy, then add pace.

### Step and pause

Take one step into your plant with the disc in the reach-back, and pause with the front foot on the ground before you pull. Then throw. This exaggerates the correct order: foot down first, then the throw.

### Weight-shift walk-through

Without a disc, rock slowly from the back leg to the front leg in your throwing stance, finishing balanced on the front leg with the back toe barely touching. Then add a slow arm motion. You are teaching the feel of arriving over the front foot, not past it.

## Where this fits

The chain explains most of the other backhand faults. Rounding is often what happens when the shoulders open early ([how to stop rounding your backhand](/blog/how-to-stop-rounding-disc-golf-backhand)). A reach-back that is all arm and no coil leaves the hips nothing to lead against ([disc golf reach back](/blog/disc-golf-reach-back)). And a run-up only helps if the plant and brace can stop it ([standstill vs x-step](/blog/standstill-vs-x-step-disc-golf)). If your goal is distance, start with the brace and hip lead, then see [how to throw farther in disc golf](/blog/how-to-throw-farther-disc-golf).
